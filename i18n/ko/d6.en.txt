About 90 min · Needs Day 5
@@ 5
Learn the value of each <em>action</em>, not just each state, and you can act greedily without a model of the world. Two one-line updates do this: SARSA learns the value of the policy you actually follow, Q-learning learns the value of the best policy while you follow a worse one. Put Q-learning behind a convolutional network, add a replay memory and a frozen copy of the network for targets, and you have DQN, the 2015 system that learned 49 Atari games from pixels.
@@ 6
1. From V to Q: acting without a model
@@ 7
<a href="#d5">Day 5</a> taught you to estimate the <strong>state value</strong> V(s) with TD(0). There is a catch when you try to act on it. To pick the greedy action from V you must ask, for each action, "where would this take me and what would I get?", which means summing over the transition probabilities p(s′, r | s, a). That is the model, and usually you do not have it. <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a>
@@ 8
The fix is to learn the <strong>action value</strong> Q(s, a): the expected return from being in s, taking a, and following the policy afterwards. The greedy choice becomes a lookup:
@@ 9
π(s) = argmax<sub>a</sub> Q(s, a)
@@ 10
For |S| states and |A| actions, Q is a table with |S| × |A| cells. The optimal action value satisfies its own Bellman equation, with a max where the policy used to be:
@@ 11
q<sub>*</sub>(s, a) = E[ R<sub>t+1</sub> + γ max<sub>a′</sub> q<sub>*</sub>(S<sub>t+1</sub>, a′) | S<sub>t</sub> = s, A<sub>t</sub> = a ]
@@ 12
Everything today is the generalised policy iteration of <a href="#d4">Day 4</a> applied to Q: evaluate with a TD update, improve by being ε-greedy with respect to the new Q, repeat after every step.
@@ 13
2. SARSA: on-policy TD control
@@ 14
Take the TD(0) update, replace V(s) with Q(s, a), and bootstrap from the action you actually take next. That is <strong>SARSA</strong>, named after the five things its update touches: S<sub>t</sub>, A<sub>t</sub>, R<sub>t+1</sub>, S<sub>t+1</sub>, A<sub>t+1</sub>. <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a>
@@ 15
Check one step by hand. Q(s, a) = −5, you receive r = −1, land in s′, and your ε-greedy policy picks a′ with Q(s′, a′) = −3. With γ = 1 the target is −4, the TD error is −4 − (−5) = +1, and with α = 0.5 the new value is <strong>−4.5</strong>. The terminal state has Q = 0, so the last update of an episode uses the bare reward.
@@ 16
SARSA is <strong>on-policy</strong>: the Q it learns is the value of the policy that generates the data, exploration included. With ε-greedy behaviour, Q(s, a) means "take a, then keep acting ε-greedily", stumbles and all. It converges to q<sub>*</sub> if every state–action pair is visited infinitely often and the policy becomes greedy in the limit, for example with ε = 1/t. Rummery and Niranjan introduced it in 1994 as "modified connectionist Q-learning"; the name SARSA is Sutton's. <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a>
@@ 17
3. Q-learning: off-policy, and the max
@@ 18
Change one symbol. Instead of bootstrapping from the action you will take, bootstrap from the <em>best</em> action available in s′:
@@ 19
Q(S<sub>t</sub>, A<sub>t</sub>) ← Q(S<sub>t</sub>, A<sub>t</sub>) + α [ R<sub>t+1</sub> + γ max<sub>a</sub> Q(S<sub>t+1</sub>, a) − Q(S<sub>t</sub>, A<sub>t</sub>) ]
@@ 20
This is <strong>Q-learning</strong>, from Chris Watkins's 1989 thesis, with the convergence proof completed by Watkins and Dayan in 1992. <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a> Same hand example, but now look at all four values in s′, say [−3, −6, −2, −9]. The max is −2, the target −3, the error +2, the new value −5 + 0.5 × 2 = <strong>−4</strong>. The ε-greedy policy may well take the −3 action next; Q-learning does not care.
@@ 21
That indifference is the point. Q-learning is <strong>off-policy</strong>: it learns about a <strong>target policy</strong> (the greedy one) from data generated by a different <strong>behaviour policy</strong> (ε-greedy, or anything that keeps visiting every pair). The learned Q "directly approximates q<sub>*</sub>, the optimal action-value function, independent of the policy being followed." <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a> One consequence matters in section 7: because the update does not depend on who chose the action, Q-learning can learn from transitions recorded by an old version of the agent. <a class="src" href="https://spinningup.openai.com/en/latest/spinningup/rl_intro2.html">OpenAI 2018</a>
@@ 22
Only with fully greedy behaviour do the two targets coincide. With any exploration they learn different things.
@@ 23
Two hikers learn a ridge path. The SARSA hiker rates each step by what happened on the steps after it, including the times a gust (ε) made them stagger, so the edge gets a bad rating and they drift inland. The Q-learning hiker rates each step by the best next step that exists, as if every future step will be perfect, so the edge gets a good rating: it is the shortest route. Both answer their own question correctly. Only one asks the question that matches how they actually walk.
@@ 24
4. Cliff walking: one grid, two answers
@@ 25
Sutton and Barto's <strong>cliff-walking</strong> task is a 4 × 12 grid: start bottom left, goal bottom right, and the ten cells between them are a cliff. Every step costs −1; stepping into the cliff costs −100 and sends you back to the start. No discounting, ε-greedy with ε = 0.1. <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a> The optimal route hugs the edge: 13 steps, return −13.
@@ 26
Q-learning learns exactly that route. But the agent is still ε-greedy while learning, so one time in forty a random "down" at the edge costs 100, and its online return settles near −50 per episode. SARSA learns the longer, safer route through the top rows; random actions there rarely hurt, and its return settles near −25. "Although Q-learning actually learns the values of the optimal policy, its online performance is worse than that of Sarsa." <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a> A replication with α = 0.5 over 50 runs gives −27.4 for SARSA and −50.9 for Q-learning over episodes 100–500; the book publishes only the curve. If ε were decayed to zero, both would end on the edge path.
@@ 27
Try it: press "Run 500", then switch the path selector. Then raise ε to 0.3 and run again: the gap widens because SARSA moves even further from the cliff.
@@ 28
Notice that Q-learning's path (13 arrows along the cliff) appears within a few hundred episodes and never changes, yet its reward curve sits below SARSA's and is spikier: each spike is an ε-step off the cliff. SARSA's path runs through the top row and is about 17 steps (it may still wiggle after 500 episodes; run more), and its curve is higher and smoother. Q-learning is right about the optimal policy; SARSA is right about the policy you are actually running.
@@ 29
You are walking along a wall next to a drop, and a friend keeps poking you for fun. One walker thinks "if nobody poked me, the fastest way is right along the edge", walks the edge, and falls every time they are poked. The other thinks "I know I get poked, so I will stay a bit back from the edge", takes a few more steps, and never falls. The first knows the perfect route. The second gets home with fewer bruises.

5. Expected SARSA and the maximisation bias
@@ 31
SARSA's target uses one sampled next action, which adds noise. <strong>Expected SARSA</strong> averages over what the policy would do:
@@ 32
Removing that variance allows a much larger step size: on cliff walking it works even at α = 1 and beats SARSA at every α tried. If the target policy is greedy while the behaviour explores, the sum becomes a max and Expected SARSA "is exactly Q-learning". <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a>
@@ 33
The max has a cost. Taking the maximum of several noisy estimates as an estimate of the maximum true value gives a positive bias, the <strong>maximisation bias</strong>. <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a> Check a tiny case: two actions, both with true value 0, each estimated with noise of +1 or −1 with equal chance. The max of the two estimates is +1 unless both are −1, which happens one time in four, so its expected value is 0.75 × 1 + 0.25 × (−1) = <strong>+0.5</strong> while the true max is 0. Q-learning feeds that inflated number into every target. In the book's two-state example, where one action leads to many noisy sub-actions with mean −0.1, ε-greedy Q-learning takes the bad action far more than the 5% that exploration alone explains, and still too often after many episodes. <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a>
@@ 34
<strong>Double Q-learning</strong>, due to Hado van Hasselt in 2010, keeps two tables and uses one to choose and the other to evaluate: <a class="src" href="https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2010/hash/091d584fced301b442654dd8c23b3fc9-Abstract.html">van Hasselt 2010</a>
@@ 35
Q<sub>1</sub>(S, A) ← Q<sub>1</sub>(S, A) + α [ R + γ Q<sub>2</sub>(S′, argmax<sub>a</sub> Q<sub>1</sub>(S′, a)) − Q<sub>1</sub>(S, A) ]
@@ 36
With probability 0.5 you run this, otherwise the mirror image; behaviour is ε-greedy in Q<sub>1</sub> + Q<sub>2</sub>. In the tiny case, if Q<sub>1</sub> picks the action whose estimate happened to be +1, Q<sub>2</sub>'s independent estimate of that action still averages 0: the bias is gone. It doubles memory, not computation, and on the two-state example it is "essentially unaffected" by the bias. <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a>
@@ 37
6. Function approximation and the deadly triad
@@ 38
A table works for 48 cells. An Atari screen, after the preprocessing in section 8, is 84 × 84 × 4 = 28,224 numbers with 256 values each; no two rows of that table would ever be visited twice. So you replace the table by a network Q(s, a; θ) and turn the update into a gradient step on the squared TD error, treating the target as a constant:
@@ 39
θ ← θ + α [ R + γ max<sub>a</sub> Q(S′, a; θ) − Q(S, A; θ) ] ∇<sub>θ</sub> Q(S, A; θ)
@@ 40
This is a <strong>semi-gradient</strong> method: the target also depends on θ, and the gradient ignores that. A new problem appears that tables never had. Sutton and Barto name three ingredients, the <strong>deadly triad</strong>: <strong>function approximation</strong>, <strong>bootstrapping</strong>, and <strong>off-policy training</strong>. Combine all three and the estimates can diverge to infinity. "If any two elements of the deadly triad are present, but not all three, then instability can be avoided." <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a> Tabular Q-learning bootstraps off-policy but does not approximate: safe. Monte Carlo with a network does not bootstrap: safe. A neural Q-learner has all three.
@@ 41
The DQN paper describes the same danger from the practitioner's side: consecutive frames are correlated samples, small changes to Q change the policy and so the data distribution, and Q and its target share parameters, so an update moves the thing it is chasing. <a class="src" href="https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/dqn/DQNNaturePaper.pdf">Mnih 2015</a> The two fixes in section 8, experience replay and a target network, attack exactly those problems.
@@ 42
<strong>"Q-learning with ε-greedy exploration and SARSA are the same algorithm."</strong> Same data, different target: the actual next action versus the max. On cliff walking they end up on different paths, 25 points apart.
@@ 43
<strong>"Off-policy means unstable."</strong> Tabular Q-learning is off-policy and provably converges. Instability needs all three legs of the triad.
@@ 44
<strong>"The target network is a second network being trained."</strong> It is a copy of the online network, overwritten every C steps. Nothing trains it.
@@ 45
<strong>"Reward clipping and error clipping are the same trick."</strong> Reward clipping changes the signal (every reward becomes −1, 0 or +1), so the agent cannot tell a big score from a small one. Error clipping bounds the loss gradient and leaves rewards alone.
@@ 46
<strong>"DQN was one network that played all 49 games."</strong> One architecture and one set of hyperparameters, but the weights were reset and trained separately per game.
@@ 47
7. DQN: from a 2013 preprint to the cover of Nature
@@ 48
In December 2013 Volodymyr Mnih and colleagues at DeepMind posted "Playing Atari with Deep Reinforcement Learning": a convolutional network reads raw pixels and outputs Q-values, Q-learning trains it from a replay memory, and the same recipe runs on seven games for 10 million frames each. It beat all earlier RL methods on six of seven games and a human expert on three: Breakout (168 points versus the human's 31), Enduro (470 versus 368) and Pong (20 versus −3). <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.5602">Mnih 2013</a> That network had two convolutional layers of 16 and 32 filters, a 256-unit hidden layer, and no target network.
@@ 49
The <em>Nature</em> paper of 26 February 2015 scaled it up: 49 games, 50 million frames each (about 38 days of play), a third convolutional layer, a 512-unit layer, and the target network, with one architecture and one set of hyperparameters throughout. Scores are reported as a <strong>human-normalised score</strong>, 100 × (DQN − random) / (human − random), the human being a professional tester after about two hours of practice. DQN beat the best existing RL methods on 43 of 49 games and reached at least 75% of the human score on 29. <a class="src" href="https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/dqn/DQNNaturePaper.pdf">Mnih 2015</a> The spread is wide: Breakout scored 401.2 against a human 31.8 and random 1.7, about 1,327% of human; Pong 132%; Montezuma's Revenge, which needs long exploration before any reward, 0.0, the same as random play. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.06461">van Hasselt 2015</a> (Per-game numbers are the Nature results as reprinted in the Double DQN paper; the Nature tables are images.)

@@ 100
Read the top row left to right. The <strong>emulator</strong> produces 210 × 160 colour frames at 60 Hz. <strong>Preprocessing</strong> φ takes the per-pixel maximum over two consecutive frames to remove sprite flicker, converts to luminance, rescales to 84 × 84, and stacks the four most recent frames, so the input is 84 × 84 × 4 and the network can see motion. The <strong>online network</strong> is three convolutional layers, 32 filters of 8 × 8 with stride 4, 64 of 4 × 4 with stride 2, 64 of 3 × 3 with stride 1 (feature maps 20 × 20, 9 × 9, 7 × 7), then 512 rectifier units and a linear output with one unit per legal action, 4 to 18 depending on the game. One forward pass values every action; ε-greedy picks one, with ε annealed from 1.0 to 0.1 over the first million frames, and the action is repeated for 4 frames (<strong>frame skip</strong>), so the agent decides 15 times a second. <a class="src" href="https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/dqn/DQNNaturePaper.pdf">Mnih 2015</a> <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a>
@@ 101
Now the bottom row. Every transition (φ<sub>t</sub>, a<sub>t</sub>, r<sub>t</sub>, φ<sub>t+1</sub>), reward clipped to −1, 0 or +1, goes into the <strong>replay memory</strong>, which keeps the most recent million. Every 4 agent steps a <strong>minibatch of 32</strong> is drawn uniformly. For each sample the <strong>target network</strong>, a copy of the online network refreshed only every C = 10,000 updates and frozen in between, computes y<sub>j</sub> = r<sub>j</sub> + γ max<sub>a′</sub> Q̂(φ<sub>j+1</sub>, a′; θ⁻), or just r<sub>j</sub> at the end of an episode. The loss is the squared gap between y<sub>j</sub> and the online Q(φ<sub>j</sub>, a<sub>j</sub>; θ), with the error clipped to [−1, 1], which "further improved the stability"; <strong>RMSProp</strong> takes one step on θ with learning rate 0.00025, γ = 0.99, and the gradient reaches only the output unit of the action taken. <a class="src" href="https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/dqn/DQNNaturePaper.pdf">Mnih 2015</a> <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.06461">van Hasselt 2015</a> Replay breaks the correlation between consecutive samples and lets each experience teach the network many times; the target network stops an update from moving its own target. Both need an off-policy learner, because replayed actions were chosen by an older policy, which is why the algorithm inside DQN is Q-learning and not SARSA.

Systems view: what runs where
@@ 107
One architecture, 49 separate training runs, one per game. Each run plays 50 million frames, about 38 days of game time; at frame skip 4 that is 12.5 million decisions and, with an update every 4 decisions, about 3.1 million gradient steps on minibatches of 32, so each stored transition is replayed roughly eight times before it leaves the million-entry buffer. The target network is copied about 310 times per run. <a class="src" href="https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/dqn/DQNNaturePaper.pdf">Mnih 2015</a> <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.06461">van Hasselt 2015</a> Wall-clock time per game is not reported; the update frequency, target period and learning rate are the Nature values as restated in the Double DQN paper. <a href="#d7">Day 7</a> shows how A3C dropped the replay memory in favour of many parallel environments on CPU cores.
@@ 108
Going deeper (optional): why the target network is not cheating
@@ 109
Between two copies the targets y<sub>j</sub> are fixed numbers, so the learning loop is ordinary supervised regression: inputs φ<sub>j</sub>, labels y<sub>j</sub>, squared loss. Every 10,000 updates the labels are regenerated from the improved network, which is one approximate step of value iteration (<a href="#d4">Day 4</a>). DQN is fitted value iteration with a neural regressor, interleaved with data collection. The deadly triad has not gone away; the copy slows the feedback from Q to its own target enough that, in practice, Atari stopped diverging. Double DQN later showed the max still overestimates in every one of the 49 games.

Q1. What is the only difference between the SARSA and Q-learning updates?
@@ 111
SARSA uses a learning rate; Q-learning does not
@@ 112
SARSA learns V(s); Q-learning learns Q(s, a)
@@ 113
SARSA bootstraps from the next action actually taken; Q-learning bootstraps from the max over next actions
@@ 114
SARSA needs the transition model; Q-learning does not
@@ 115
Answer 3. Both learn Q(s, a) with a step size and without a model. SARSA's target uses Q(S′, A′) where A′ is the behaviour policy's next action; Q-learning's uses max<sub>a</sub> Q(S′, a). That one symbol makes SARSA on-policy and Q-learning off-policy.
@@ 116
Q2. On cliff walking with ε = 0.1, Q-learning learns the optimal cliff-edge path yet SARSA scores better online (about −25 versus −50 per episode). Why?
@@ 117
Q-learning's greedy path runs next to the cliff, so ε-random actions regularly cost 100; SARSA's values include those stumbles and steer it to a safer path
@@ 118
SARSA converges faster because its update has lower variance
@@ 119
Q-learning never finds the goal because the max over-estimates the cliff cells
@@ 120
SARSA uses a larger step size by default
@@ 121
Answer 1. Q-learning evaluates the greedy policy, which is never pushed off the cliff; the agent running ε-greedy is. SARSA evaluates the ε-greedy policy itself, so cells near the cliff look bad and it walks through the top rows. With ε decayed to zero both would take the edge.
@@ 122
Q3. Two actions both have true value 0; each estimate is +1 or −1 with equal chance. What is the expected value of the max of the two estimates?
@@ 123
Answer 4. The max is −1 only when both estimates are −1 (probability 1/4), otherwise +1: 0.75 × 1 + 0.25 × (−1) = 0.5. That positive gap is the maximisation bias; Double Q-learning removes it by choosing with one estimate and evaluating with another.

Q4. Which combination is the "deadly triad"?
@@ 125
Exploration, discounting, and stochastic rewards
@@ 126
Function approximation, bootstrapping, and off-policy training
@@ 127
Replay memory, target network, and reward clipping
@@ 128
Large step size, small batch, and no discounting
@@ 129
Answer 2. Any two of the three are safe; all three together can make value estimates diverge. Option 3 lists DQN's remedies, not the disease.

Q6. What is the target network in DQN?
@@ 137
A copy of the online network's weights, refreshed every C = 10,000 updates and frozen in between, used only to compute targets
@@ 138
A second network trained on the same minibatches with a smaller learning rate
@@ 139
A network that predicts the human player's score for normalisation
@@ 140
The network from the 2013 preprint, kept as a baseline
@@ 141
