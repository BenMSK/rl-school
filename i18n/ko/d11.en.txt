@@ 5
Reward-maximising agents only improve on what they have already stumbled into. When rewards are rare, you have to manufacture a reason to go somewhere new: a bonus for novelty, a memory of where you have been, or an opponent who is exactly as good as you. Self-play is the cleanest version of that last trick, and it stops being clean the moment more than two players, or cooperation, enter the game.
@@ 6
1. Hard exploration: the game that scored zero
@@ 7
By 2015 DQN (<a href="#d6">Day 6</a>, <a href="#p-dqn">the DQN review</a>) was superhuman on dozens of Atari games, yet on Montezuma's Revenge it scored 0.02 points after 200 million frames and saw only two rooms. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.01868">Bellemare 2016</a> The first level is a maze of 24 rooms, and the first points need a long chain: climb down a ladder, jump a skull, take a key, climb back, open a door, and one wrong step kills you. Random actions never complete that chain, so the agent never sees a reward, so there is no gradient. This is <strong>hard exploration</strong>: rewards so sparse or so far away that undirected noise cannot reach them.
@@ 8
ε-greedy (<a href="#d6">Day 6</a>) and entropy bonuses (<a href="#d7">Day 7</a>) are <strong>undirected</strong>: they explore equally everywhere, including places visited a thousand times. In a 30-cell corridor with one reward at the end, ε-greedy with all Q-values at zero is a random walk, which needs on the order of 30² ≈ 900 steps to reach the end. Montezuma is that corridor with ladders and skulls.
@@ 9
2. Count-based bonuses and pseudo-counts
@@ 10
The oldest fix is to pay the agent for visiting rare states. Keep a table <strong>N(s)</strong> of how many times each state has been visited and add an <strong>exploration bonus</strong> to the reward:
@@ 11
A fresh state pays β; one visited 100 times pays β/10. Because the bonus is a reward, Q-learning propagates it backwards and the agent learns to <em>steer toward</em> the frontier rather than wander. The catch is the table: in pixel space every frame is new, N(s) = 1 forever, and the bonus is flat.
@@ 12
Bellemare and colleagues derived a <strong>pseudo-count</strong> from a density model ρ over frames instead: if ρ(x) is the model's probability of frame x and ρ′(x) the probability after training on x one more time, then N̂(x) = ρ(x)(1 − ρ′(x)) / (ρ′(x) − ρ(x)) behaves like a visit count, and they used the bonus β(N̂ + 0.01)^−1/2 with β = 0.05. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.01868">Bellemare 2016</a> Plugged into DQN, the agent visited <strong>15 rooms</strong> of Montezuma within 50 million frames against 2 without the bonus, averaging 2,461 points at that stage, 3,439 at 100 million frames, with a best run of 6,600. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.01868">Bellemare 2016</a>
@@ 13
Try it: the corridor below has one reward at the far end. Run 200 steps with plain ε-greedy, reset, then run 200 steps with the count bonus switched on.
@@ 14
Notice the shape of the visit counts. ε-greedy piles them near the start (cell 15 is the furthest it gets in 200 steps; it needs about 1,100 steps to touch cell 29 once). The count bonus spreads visits down the corridor, reaches the reward at step 82 and keeps returning. The policy did not change; only what counts as reward.
@@ 15
Imagine a huge house where the only candy is in the last room. Wandering at random, you keep ending up in the same three rooms by the front door. Now every room has a sticker machine: a whole sticker the first time you enter, half a sticker the fourth time, almost nothing after that. Suddenly the far rooms are the interesting ones, and on the way you bump into the candy. RND, below, is a sticker machine that still works when every room looks slightly different each visit.

3. Curiosity: ICM and RND
@@ 17
A second idea replaces counts with <strong>prediction error</strong>: be curious about what you cannot yet predict. The <strong>Intrinsic Curiosity Module</strong> (ICM) trains a forward model to predict the next state's features from φ(s<sub>t</sub>) and a<sub>t</sub>, and pays the agent the error:
@@ 18
The trick is in φ: an <strong>inverse model</strong> trains it to predict a<sub>t</sub> from (φ(s<sub>t</sub>), φ(s<sub>t+1</sub>)), so φ keeps only what the agent's actions can influence and ignores leaves blowing in the wind. On sparse-reward VizDoom ICM reached a 100% mean score where plain A3C got 0%, and in Super Mario with the game reward removed it crossed more than 30% of level 1 on curiosity alone. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.05363">Pathak 2017</a>
@@ 19
<strong>Random Network Distillation</strong> (RND, <a href="#p-rnd">the RND review</a>) is simpler and became the standard. Take two networks of the same shape. The <strong>target</strong> f is randomly initialised and frozen forever; the <strong>predictor</strong> f̂ is trained by mean-squared error to copy f's output on observations the agent has seen. The intrinsic reward is the copying error:
@@ 20
On familiar observations the predictor has learned the target's output, so the bonus is small; on a new one the error is large. Because the target is a fixed deterministic function, a flickering screen is not forever surprising: the predictor can learn it and the bonus decays. That kills the <strong>noisy-TV problem</strong> of pixel-prediction curiosity, where any source of randomness is infinitely interesting. Observations are whitened and clipped to [−5, 5], the bonus is divided by its running standard deviation, and PPO gets <strong>two value heads</strong>: episodic extrinsic, non-episodic intrinsic. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.12894">Burda 2018</a>
@@ 21
After roughly 1.97 billion frames RND averaged <strong>8,152</strong> on Montezuma's Revenge against 2,497 for plain PPO and 400 for dynamics-prediction curiosity, above the <strong>average human score of 4,753</strong>, with a best return of 17,500 and the best agents finding <strong>22 of the 24 rooms</strong> on level 1; a recurrent variant with 1,024 parallel environments averaged 10,070. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.12894">Burda 2018</a> It was the first agent past the average human without demonstrations.
@@ 22
<strong>"Curiosity means rewarding surprising pixels."</strong> Raw pixel-prediction error gets stuck staring at anything random. ICM predicts only controllable features; RND predicts a fixed deterministic function, so noise becomes learnable and stops paying. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.12894">Burda 2018</a>
@@ 23
<strong>"RND trains both networks."</strong> Only the predictor trains. If the target also moved, the error would never settle and the bonus would mean nothing.
@@ 24
<strong>"Go-Explore is another intrinsic reward."</strong> It uses no bonus at all. It is an explicit archive of places plus a rule for returning to them.
@@ 25
<strong>"AlphaGo learned Go without humans."</strong> The 2016 AlphaGo started from human expert games; the tabula-rasa system is <em>AlphaGo Zero</em>, a year later. <a class="src" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/nature24270">Silver 2017</a>
@@ 26
4. Go-Explore and Agent57: remember, return, then explore
@@ 27
Go-Explore's authors diagnosed two failures bonuses cannot cure. <strong>Detachment</strong>: the bonus in an area is consumed, the agent drifts elsewhere, and forgets how to get back to the frontier. <strong>Derailment</strong>: even if it remembers, the noise needed to find new things knocks it off the long, precise path back. <a class="src" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-03157-9">Ecoffet 2021</a>
@@ 28
<strong>Go-Explore</strong> separates the two jobs. It keeps an <strong>archive of cells</strong>, where a cell is a frame downscaled to 11 × 8 greyscale pixels with 8 intensity levels, so that many nearby frames share a cell. Each iteration: pick a cell (favouring rarely chosen ones), <strong>return</strong> to it with no noise (in 2019, by restoring the emulator state), then <strong>explore</strong> randomly from there, adding new cells and better routes to the archive. Phase 2 <strong>robustifies</strong> the best trajectories into a policy by imitation learning that starts near the end and works backwards. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.10995">Ecoffet 2019</a>
@@ 29
Without game-specific knowledge it averaged <strong>43,763</strong> on Montezuma's Revenge, against a previous intrinsic-motivation best of about 11,500 mean and a human expert's roughly 34,900. With hand-written cell features (room, level, keys) it averaged 666,474, and one run reached 18,003,200 points, past the human world record of 1,219,200. On Pitfall, where every previous RL method scored 0 or less, it averaged <strong>59,494</strong>. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.10995">Ecoffet 2019</a> The Nature version replaced state restoration with a goal-conditioned policy, solved every previously unsolved Atari game, and transferred to a sparse-reward robot task. <a class="src" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-03157-9">Ecoffet 2021</a>
@@ 30
One line on <strong>Agent57</strong> (2020): episodic novelty × RND-style lifelong novelty, a family of policies from curious to greedy, and a bandit choosing among them gave the first agent above the human baseline on all 57 Atari games. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.13350">Badia 2020</a>

5. Self-play: from TD-Gammon to AlphaZero
@@ 51
Board games have the opposite problem: the reward is clear but you need an opponent at your level, and a too-strong one teaches nothing. <strong>Self-play</strong> has the agent play copies of itself, so the opponent is always exactly as good as you and improves as fast: a free, automatic curriculum.
@@ 52
The idea predates deep learning. Gerald Tesauro's <strong>TD-Gammon</strong> (1992–1995) trained a small multilayer network by TD(λ) from self-play alone, starting from random weights, about 200,000 games for the 40-hidden-unit version; version 2.1 finished a 40-game series near parity with Bill Robertie, one of the world's best players. <a class="src" href="https://www.bkgm.com/articles/tesauro/tdl.html">Tesauro 1995</a>
@@ 53
<strong>AlphaGo</strong> (<a href="#p-alphago">the AlphaGo review</a>) brought the recipe to Go with three parts: a <strong>policy network</strong>, trained first on human expert games and then by self-play policy gradient; a <strong>value network</strong> predicting the winner from self-play positions; and <strong>Monte Carlo tree search</strong> (MCTS, <a href="#d9">Day 9</a>), which uses the policy to choose which moves to look at and the value network plus fast rollouts to score positions. It won 99.8% of games against other Go programs and beat the European champion Fan Hui <strong>5–0</strong> in October 2015. <a class="src" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/nature16961">Silver 2016</a> In March 2016 it beat Lee Sedol <strong>4–1</strong> in Seoul before more than 200 million viewers; its move 37 in game two had a one-in-10,000 chance of being played by a human. <a class="src" href="https://deepmind.google/research/breakthroughs/alphago/">DeepMind 2016</a>
@@ 54
Then DeepMind removed the humans. <strong>AlphaGo Zero</strong> (October 2017) starts from random play with only the rules, uses <strong>one network</strong> with a policy head and a value head, and drops the rollouts: MCTS scores leaves with the value head alone. The search becomes the teacher: at every position MCTS produces a visit distribution π better than the raw policy p, and the network is trained to make p match π and v match the game result z. After <strong>3 days</strong> it beat the version that beat Lee Sedol <strong>100–0</strong>; after <strong>40 days</strong> and about 5 million games on 4 TPUs it passed AlphaGo Master, the strongest previous version. <a class="src" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/nature24270">Silver 2017</a> <a class="src" href="https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphago-zero-starting-from-scratch/">DeepMind 2017</a>
@@ 55
<strong>AlphaZero</strong> (preprint December 2017, Science December 2018) ran the same algorithm, unchanged, on chess and shogi. From random play it overtook Stockfish after <strong>4 hours</strong>, the shogi program Elmo after <strong>2 hours</strong>, and AlphaGo Lee after <strong>30 hours</strong>; against Stockfish it won 155 and lost 6 of 1,000 games. It searched about <strong>60 thousand positions per second</strong> against Stockfish's <strong>60 million</strong>, a thousand times fewer, because the network makes the search selective. It played on 4 first-generation TPUs and 44 CPU cores. <a class="src" href="https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphazero-shedding-new-light-on-chess-shogi-and-go/">DeepMind 2018</a>
@@ 56
Try it: the widget below is the MCTS half of that loop on tic-tac-toe, with random rollouts instead of a value head. Set the simulations per move and play 100 games, or press Sweep for the whole curve.
@@ 57
Notice the win rate rising with simulations: against a random opponent from about 83% at 10 simulations to about 96% at 500; against the 1-ply opponent from about 61% to 95%. The player never changes; it only thinks longer. In AlphaZero the network replaces the rollouts and the improved search becomes the training target, so thinking longer today makes the network stronger tomorrow.

6. Multi-agent RL: why it is harder than it looks
@@ 63
Two-player zero-sum games are the easy case of multi-agent learning. Four things break when you leave it.
@@ 64
<strong>Non-stationarity.</strong> Every method so far assumes the environment does not change while you learn. Other learning agents are part of your environment, so, as the MADDPG paper puts it, "the environment becomes non-stationary from the perspective of any individual agent". <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02275">Lowe 2017</a> A replayed transition from last month was generated against an opponent who no longer exists; the Markov assumption behind Q-learning and replay quietly fails.
@@ 65
<strong>What "optimal" means.</strong> With several players the standard answer is a <strong>Nash equilibrium</strong>: a strategy for every player such that nobody can do better by changing only their own. In rock-paper-scissors it is one third each; any deviation can be exploited. In two-player zero-sum games the equilibrium is the minimax solution and self-play converges toward it, which is why AlphaZero's recipe works. In <strong>general-sum</strong> games, with teammates or negotiation, there can be many equilibria with different payoffs, and "play the equilibrium" is not a complete instruction. That is why Cicero, below, models how <em>humans</em> play instead of solving the game.
@@ 66
<strong>Who sees what.</strong> <strong>Centralised training, decentralised execution</strong> (CTDE): during training each agent's critic sees every agent's observations and actions, which makes its world stationary again; at execution each actor uses only its own observation, so the team runs with no central brain. MADDPG is the actor-critic (<a href="#d7">Day 7</a>) version. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02275">Lowe 2017</a>
@@ 67
<strong>Who you train against.</strong> Playing only your latest self can cycle: beat A with B, forget A, relearn A. <strong>Population-based training</strong> keeps 10 to 80 agents, periodically has the bottom 20% copy the weights and hyperparameters of the top 20%, then perturbs the hyperparameters by ×0.8 or ×1.2; it lifted UNREAL on DMLab from 93% to 106% human-normalised. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.09846">Jaderberg 2017</a> AlphaStar's <strong>league</strong> adds <strong>exploiter</strong> agents whose only job is to find weaknesses in the main agents. <a class="src" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1724-z">Vinyals 2019</a>
@@ 68
Three systems show the scale this took; as of October 2026 they remain the landmarks:
@@ 69
<strong>OpenAI Five</strong> (Dota 2, five against five). One run, <strong>180 days of training spread over 10 months</strong> (June 2018 to April 2019) with more than 20 "surgeries" that changed model or environment without restarting. It beat the world champions OG <strong>2–0</strong> on 13 April 2019, then won <strong>99.4% of 7,257</strong> public games in the four-day OpenAI Five Arena, with a 17-hero pool. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06680">Berner 2019</a>
@@ 70
<strong>AlphaStar</strong> (StarCraft II). Seeded by imitating human games (already better than 84% of players), then trained in the league; Grandmaster in all three races, above <strong>99.8%</strong> of ranked players on Battle.net, playing anonymously with a human camera view and at most 22 actions per 5 seconds. <a class="src" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1724-z">Vinyals 2019</a>
@@ 71
<strong>Cicero</strong> (Diplomacy, seven players, natural-language negotiation). A 2.7-billion-parameter dialogue model fine-tuned on more than 40,000 human games, plus a planner (piKL) that predicts human moves and improves on them while staying human-like. Over 40 games on webDiplomacy.net it scored more than double the average and placed in the <strong>top 10%</strong>. <a class="src" href="https://ai.meta.com/blog/cicero-ai-negotiates-persuades-and-cooperates-with-people/">Meta 2022</a>
