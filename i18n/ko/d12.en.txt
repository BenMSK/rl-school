@@ 5
A language model is already a policy: its next-token distribution. Everything today is one recipe applied three times. Sample responses, score them (with a model of human preference, a verifier, or the outcome of a task), and push probability toward the responses that beat a baseline, while staying close enough to the starting model that the scores still mean something.
@@ 6
1. Language as an MDP
@@ 7
Take <a href="#d8">Day 8</a>'s PPO and swap the robot for a Transformer. The <strong>state</strong> is the prompt plus every token generated so far. The <strong>action</strong> is the next token. The transition appends it. The <strong>reward</strong> arrives once, at the end of the response, and is zero everywhere else. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740">Ahmadian 2024</a> (<a href="https://benmsk.github.io/llm-school/#d8">LLM School Day 8</a> tells the same story from the LLM side.)
@@ 8
Stated like that, it is a nasty RL problem: 50,000 to 150,000 discrete actions, a horizon of up to 10,000 tokens, and one sparse number for the whole trace. In Week 1 that combination meant "does not learn".
@@ 9
What saves it is the starting point. After SFT, a Llama model puts about 60% of its probability on the top token and over 90% on the top 16, so gradient variance is low. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740">Ahmadian 2024</a> Because the reward only lands at the end, you can even treat the <em>whole completion</em> as one action; it works as well as token-level modelling. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740">Ahmadian 2024</a>
@@ 10
Three eras, one recipe, three judges. <strong>RLHF</strong> (2017–2023): the judge is a model trained to imitate human taste. <strong>RLVR</strong>, RL with verifiable rewards (2024–): the judge is a program, the answer key at the back of the maths book. <strong>Agentic RL</strong> (2025–): the judge is the world: did the multi-turn, tool-using episode reach the goal? Each era swapped a judge that could be fooled for one that is harder to fool.
@@ 11
2. RLHF: InstructGPT
@@ 12
Learning a reward from pairwise human comparisons dates to 2017. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741">Christiano 2017</a> InstructGPT (March 2022) applied it to GPT-3 in three steps: SFT on about 13k labeler-written demonstrations; a <strong>reward model</strong>, where labelers rank K = 4 to 9 sampled outputs for about 33k prompts and a 6B model learns a scalar r<sub>θ</sub>(x, y) (6B even for the 175B policy); then PPO on about 31k prompts with a KL penalty to the SFT model. <a class="src" href="https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.02155">Ouyang 2022</a>
@@ 13
The reward model is trained with the <strong>Bradley–Terry loss</strong>: the probability that the human preferred y<sub>w</sub> over y<sub>l</sub> is a sigmoid of the score difference, and the loss is its negative log.
@@ 14
loss(θ) = −1/C(K,2) · E[ log σ( r<sub>θ</sub>(x, y<sub>w</sub>) − r<sub>θ</sub>(x, y<sub>l</sub>) ) ]
@@ 15
The 1/C(K,2) divides by the number of pairs a ranking of K yields, so every prompt counts equally. By hand: scores 2.0 and 0.5 give σ(1.5) = 0.818 and loss −ln 0.818 = 0.201; backwards, σ(−1.5) = 0.182 and loss 1.70. <a class="src" href="https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.02155">Ouyang 2022</a> The RL objective (PPO-ptx) is
@@ 16
objective(φ) = E<sub>(x,y)~π<sub>φ</sub></sub>[ r<sub>θ</sub>(x,y) − β · log( π<sub>φ</sub>(y|x) / π<sup>SFT</sup>(y|x) ) ] + γ · E<sub>x~pretrain</sub>[ log π<sub>φ</sub>(x) ]
@@ 17
The first term is the reward minus a <strong>KL penalty</strong> toward the frozen SFT model, weighted by <strong>β</strong>: larger β means less reward hacking but less improvement. The γ term mixes in pretraining loss against the "alignment tax"; plain PPO has γ = 0. <a class="src" href="https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.02155">Ouyang 2022</a>
@@ 18
Count the networks: the <strong>policy</strong> (trained), the frozen <strong>reference</strong>, the frozen <strong>reward model</strong>, and PPO's <strong>value model</strong> (trained). Four copies of a large model, two with optimizer state; OpenRLHF places "the Actor, Reward, Reference, and Critic models on separate GPUs". <a class="src" href="https://github.com/OpenRLHF/OpenRLHF">OpenRLHF 2026</a> The payoff: "outputs from the 1.3B parameter InstructGPT model are preferred to outputs from the 175B GPT-3, despite having 100x fewer parameters". <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155">Ouyang 2022</a> See <a href="#p-instructgpt">the InstructGPT review</a>.
@@ 19
Try it: step through one RLHF cycle. Each step lights up the models that are busy and shows its numbers.
@@ 20
Notice that the reward model is trained once, before any RL, and that the only per-token signal during RL is the KL penalty; the reward lands on the last token and the value model spreads it backwards.
@@ 21
Imagine a spelling-bee judge who cannot tell you the right word but can always say which of two attempts sounded better. First you build a robot that copies her taste. Then you practise against the robot thousands of times, doing more of what it likes. One rule: you may get better, but you may not turn into a completely different kid overnight. That rule is the KL leash.

3. DPO: skip the reward model
@@ 23
<strong>Direct Preference Optimization</strong> (May 2023) starts from the same objective, max<sub>π</sub> E[r(x,y)] − β·KL(π ‖ π<sub>ref</sub>), whose optimum has a closed form: π<sub>r</sub>(y|x) = π<sub>ref</sub>(y|x)·exp(r(x,y)/β) / Z(x), where Z(x) sums over every possible response and cannot be computed. Read it backwards: r = β·log(π<sub>r</sub>/π<sub>ref</sub>) + β·log Z(x). Put that into the Bradley–Terry loss. Both responses in a pair share the prompt, so they share Z(x), and it <strong>cancels</strong>. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290">Rafailov 2023</a>
@@ 24
L<sub>DPO</sub> = −E[ log σ( β·log π<sub>θ</sub>(y<sub>w</sub>|x)/π<sub>ref</sub>(y<sub>w</sub>|x) − β·log π<sub>θ</sub>(y<sub>l</sub>|x)/π<sub>ref</sub>(y<sub>l</sub>|x) ) ]
@@ 25
Each β·log(π<sub>θ</sub>/π<sub>ref</sub>) is an <strong>implicit reward</strong>: "your language model is secretly a reward model". With β = 0.1, winner log-ratio +0.5 and loser −0.3, the argument is 0.08, σ(0.08) = 0.520, loss 0.654; DPO keeps pushing until the margin is large. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2305.18290v3">Rafailov 2023</a> Two models instead of four, no sampling, and results that match or beat PPO-RLHF on sentiment, summarisation and dialogue. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290">Rafailov 2023</a>
@@ 26
What it gives up is what made it cheap. DPO is <strong>off-policy</strong>: it learns only from the given pairs and cannot explore. Its experiments stop at 6B and flag generalisation and over-optimisation as open. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2305.18290v3">Rafailov 2023</a> In 2024 an on-policy REINFORCE variant (RLOO) beat it on TL;DR summarisation, 77.9 vs 66.6 win rate. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740">Ahmadian 2024</a> And it cannot do the next section: a verifier gives a 0/1 on a fresh sample, not a pair. See <a href="#p-dpo">the DPO review</a>.

4. RLVR and reasoning models
@@ 28
OpenAI's o1 (September 2024) was "trained with large-scale reinforcement learning to reason using chain of thought", and that is all its system card says. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16720">OpenAI 2024</a> The recipe became public in January 2025 with DeepSeek-R1 (<a href="https://benmsk.github.io/llm-school/#d9">LLM School Day 9</a> covers reasoning models from the user's side). <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948">DeepSeek-AI 2025</a>
@@ 29
<strong>DeepSeek-R1-Zero</strong> is pure RL on the DeepSeek-V3 base model: no SFT, no human-written reasoning traces. The algorithm is <strong>GRPO</strong> (Group Relative Policy Optimization), introduced in DeepSeekMath in February 2024 as "a variant of PPO" that saves memory. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300">Shao 2024</a> For each question q, sample G outputs from the old policy, score each, and use the group's own mean and standard deviation as the baseline:
@@ 30
J<sub>GRPO</sub>(θ) = E[ (1/G) Σ<sub>i=1..G</sub> ( min( ρ<sub>i</sub>A<sub>i</sub>, clip(ρ<sub>i</sub>, 1−ε, 1+ε)A<sub>i</sub> ) − β·D<sub>KL</sub>(π<sub>θ</sub> ‖ π<sub>ref</sub>) ) ]
@@ 31
ρ<sub>i</sub> = π<sub>θ</sub>(o<sub>i</sub>|q) / π<sub>old</sub>(o<sub>i</sub>|q) &nbsp;&nbsp; A<sub>i</sub> = ( r<sub>i</sub> − mean{r<sub>1</sub>..r<sub>G</sub>} ) / std{r<sub>1</sub>..r<sub>G</sub>}
@@ 32
D<sub>KL</sub> = π<sub>ref</sub>(o<sub>i</sub>|q)/π<sub>θ</sub>(o<sub>i</sub>|q) − log( π<sub>ref</sub>(o<sub>i</sub>|q)/π<sub>θ</sub>(o<sub>i</sub>|q) ) − 1
@@ 33
The clipped ratio is PPO's. New is A<sub>i</sub>: the <strong>group-relative advantage</strong> replaces the value model. In code the ratio is applied per token and averaged with a 1/|o<sub>i</sub>| per response (section 5). R1-Zero used G = 16 and β = 0.001. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12948v1">DeepSeek-AI 2025</a> The reward is a <strong>rule</strong>: accuracy (the boxed answer matches, or the code passes its tests) plus format (reasoning inside <code>&lt;think&gt;…&lt;/think&gt;</code> tags). Neural process reward models were tried and dropped. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12948">DeepSeek-AI 2026</a>
@@ 34
Try it: four answers to "What is 17 × 23?" (truth 391). Toggle correctness and format, change G, and watch the advantages.
@@ 35
Notice that rewards [2, 1, 1, 0] have mean 1.0 and population std 0.7071, so GRPO gives [+1.414, 0, 0, −1.414]: answers 2 and 3 get zero gradient although one is right and one is wrong. They are average for this group. Make every answer correct and every advantage is 0; the prompt teaches nothing, which is what DAPO's dynamic sampling throws away. (TRL uses the sample std, giving ±1.225; the R1 paper does not say which.) <a class="src" href="https://huggingface.co/docs/trl/grpo_trainer">Hugging Face 2026</a>
@@ 36
Over training, R1-Zero's AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 71.0% (86.7% with majority vote over 64 samples) and its responses grew steadily longer: it learned to think for longer. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12948v1">DeepSeek-AI 2025</a> The Nature version reports 77.9% under a different evaluation. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12948">DeepSeek-AI 2026</a> The <strong>"aha moment"</strong> is an intermediate checkpoint that writes, mid-solution, "Wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here" and re-derives. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12948v1">DeepSeek-AI 2025</a>
@@ 37
R1-Zero's traces were unreadable and mixed languages, so the released <strong>DeepSeek-R1</strong> adds a <strong>cold start</strong>: SFT on thousands of human-polished long reasoning examples, then reasoning RL with a language-consistency reward, then rejection sampling and SFT on about 800k samples, then a final RL stage for helpfulness and harmlessness. R1 scores 79.8 on AIME 2024 and 97.3 on MATH-500. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12948v1">DeepSeek-AI 2025</a> One more number matters if you do not own a 671B model: distilling R1's 800k samples into Qwen-32B by plain SFT gives AIME 72.6; R1-Zero-style RL run directly on Qwen-32B gives 47.0. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12948v1">DeepSeek-AI 2025</a> See <a href="#p-deepseek-r1">the DeepSeek-R1 review</a>.

5. GRPO's 2025–26 fixes
@@ 39
<strong>Dr. GRPO</strong> ("GRPO Done Right", March 2025) names two biases. The 1/|o<sub>i</sub>| normaliser gives a long wrong answer a smaller per-token penalty than a short one, so the policy drifts toward longer incorrect responses. Dividing by the std over-weights lopsided groups: rewards [1, 0, 0, 0] have std 0.433 and advantages [+1.73, −0.58, −0.58, −0.58], while [1, 1, 0, 0] has std 0.5 and gets [+1, +1, −1, −1], although the raw reward gaps are 0.75 and 0.5. The fix removes both terms: a constant normaliser, and A<sub>i</sub> = r<sub>i</sub> − mean. Their minimal recipe reaches 43.3% on AIME 2024 with a 7B base in about a day on 8 A100s, and they found DeepSeek-V3-Base already produces "aha"-style self-reflection before any RL. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783">Liu 2025</a>
@@ 40
<strong>DAPO</strong> (ByteDance Seed, March 2025) is an open recipe of four changes, each ablated on AIME 2024 with Qwen2.5-32B: <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476">Yu 2025</a>

6. What scaling RL compute looks like
@@ 89
Pretraining has scaling laws; until late 2025 RL post-training had folklore. "The Art of Scaling Reinforcement Learning Compute for LLMs" (October 2025; an oral at ICLR 2026 on 25 April 2026) spent over 400,000 GPU-hours of ablations on an 8B dense model and Llama-4 Scout to fit one curve: <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13786">Khatri 2025</a> <a class="src" href="https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/oral/10010608">ICLR 2026</a>
@@ 90
R<sub>C</sub> − R<sub>0</sub> = (A − R<sub>0</sub>) · 1 / ( 1 + (C<sub>mid</sub>/C)<sup>B</sup> )
@@ 91
R<sub>C</sub> is the pass rate after compute C, R<sub>0</sub> the start, <strong>A</strong> the <strong>asymptote</strong> (the ceiling this recipe can ever reach), <strong>B</strong> the exponent (how fast it gets there), C<sub>mid</sub> the half-way compute. A sigmoid, not a power law, because it fits robustly at low compute. The useful finding is which knobs move which. <strong>Moving A</strong>: the loss type (CISPO and GSPO beat DAPO), FP32 logits at the LM head (A rose from about 0.52 to 0.61), larger batches, longer generations, zero-variance filtering. <strong>Moving mostly B</strong>: asynchronous generation (PipelineRL vs PPO-off-policy: same ceiling, faster), loss aggregation, the advantage-normalisation variant, curriculum. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13786">Khatri 2025</a>
@@ 92
The <strong>ScaleRL</strong> recipe: async PipelineRL eight steps off-policy, CISPO loss, FP32 head, prompt-level loss aggregation, batch-level advantage normalisation, zero-variance filtering, dropping prompts with a historical pass rate ≥ 0.9, and a forced end-of-thinking phrase for length control. A 100,000 GPU-hour run validated it: the curve fitted on the early portion predicted the later points. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13786">Khatri 2025</a> The practical lesson: find your A on small runs first, because no amount of compute moves a recipe past its ceiling. See <a href="#p-scalerl">the ScaleRL review</a>.

7. Does RL add capability or sharpen it? And what gets hacked
@@ 94
Yue et al. (April 2025) compared base and RLVR models on <strong>pass@k</strong>, the chance that at least one of k samples is right, for k up to 1,024. RLVR wins at k = 1; at large k the base wins. On AIME 2024 at k = 1,024, 13.3% of problems were solved only by the base and 0.0% only by the RL model, and six RLVR algorithms behaved alike. Their reading: RLVR improves sampling efficiency on what the base could already do, while distillation from a stronger teacher adds new reasoning patterns. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837">Yue 2025</a> Ni et al. (October 2025) supply the theory: GRPO is "a conservative reweighting scheme bounded by the base model's distribution"; it cannot find a solution the base gives zero probability. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15990">Ni 2025</a>
@@ 95
The counter-claim is <strong>ProRL</strong> (NVIDIA, May 2025): over 2,000 RL steps on 136k verifiable problems, with a KL penalty and periodic resets of the reference policy and optimizer, a 1.5B model beat its starting point on pass@k everywhere, with pass@1 gains from +14.7% on math to +54.8% on logic puzzles. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24864">Liu 2025</a> Caveats: the start model was itself distilled, and the biggest gains are on tasks far from its training distribution. Honest summary as of October 2026: short RL runs sharpen; whether long, diverse RL with resets truly expands what a model can do is contested.
@@ 96
Whatever the judge, the policy learns to please it. <strong>Sycophancy</strong>: across five production assistants, responses matching the user's view were likelier to be preferred, and "both humans and preference models sometimes prefer convincing sycophantic responses over correct ones". <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548">Sharma 2023</a> <strong>Length</strong>: most of RLHF's reward gain comes from longer outputs, and a reward based purely on length recovers most of the improvement over SFT. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03716">Singhal 2023</a> RLVR adds format gaming and exploited verifiers. In September 2026 Anthropic's Opus 5.5 post said the company was "tightening how we filter the environments used in reinforcement learning", linking flawed environments to misaligned behaviour. <a class="src" href="https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-opus-5-5">Anthropic 2026</a>
@@ 97
Going deeper (optional): process versus outcome rewards
@@ 98
An <strong>outcome reward model</strong> (ORM) scores the final answer; a <strong>process reward model</strong> (PRM) scores every step. "Let's Verify Step by Step" (2023) found process supervision significantly beat outcome supervision on MATH, with PRM-guided search solving 78% of a test subset, using 800k human step labels (PRM800K). <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050">Lightman 2023</a> DeepSeek-R1 reports the opposite at scale: steps are hard to define and label, and the policy hacks the PRM. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12948">DeepSeek-AI 2026</a> The 2025 reasoning wave is overwhelmingly outcome-verified; for domains with no exact checker, DeepSeek-V4's report lists a generative reward model, an LLM that writes a judgement. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19348">DeepSeek-AI 2026</a>
@@ 99
8. Agentic RL: when the episode is a conversation
@@ 100
An <strong>agent episode</strong> is a conversation with tools. The policy emits tokens; some form a tool call; an environment (sandbox, API, browser, simulated user) returns an observation that is appended to the context; the policy continues; at the end the environment reports whether the goal was met. One detail every system shares: <strong>tool outputs and user turns are masked out of the loss</strong>. They are observations, not actions, and the trainer must see exactly the tokens the policy produced, which is why verl's agent loop generates at the token level rather than round-tripping through text. <a class="src" href="https://verl.readthedocs.io/en/latest/start/agentic_rl.html">verl 2026</a>
@@ 101
The benchmarks define the rewards. <strong>τ-bench</strong> (June 2024): a simulated user, domain APIs, policy rules; success means the final database state matches the goal. GPT-4o succeeded on under 50% of tasks, and its <strong>pass^k</strong>, the chance that all k trials succeed, was below 25% at k = 8 in retail. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045">Yao 2024</a> <strong>τ²-bench</strong> (June 2025) gives the user tools too, so the agent must guide a person, with "significant performance drops". <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07982">Barres 2025</a> <strong>SWE-RL</strong> (Meta, February 2025) trains on open-source software history with a rule-based reward, similarity to the real patch, and takes Llama3-70B to 41.0% on SWE-bench Verified. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18449">Wei 2025</a> <strong>Agent World Model</strong> (February 2026, ICML 2026) builds 1,000 synthetic, code-driven, database-backed environments whose inspectable state makes rewards reliable; training only on them generalised to three held-out benchmarks. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10090">Wang 2026</a> <strong>ToolVerse</strong> (July 2026) turns about 400 real MCP servers, about 4,500 tools, into environments with long-horizon tasks generated from a tool-dependency graph. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15660">Zhou 2026</a>
@@ 102
The open problem is <strong>credit assignment</strong>: one terminal reward over 20 to 50 turns and 10,000+ tokens. GRPO gives every token in a trajectory the same sign, so a successful episode's one bad tool call is rewarded too. Left to right: inference workers generate; in the agentic case an agent loop on top executes tool calls, appends observations, and records which tokens were the policy's. Trajectories (tokens, log-probabilities, loss mask, reward) go into a queue. The trainer pool recomputes log-probabilities, forms the advantage, takes a clipped step, and ships the new weights back. Synchronous: the pools take turns and each idles half the time. Asynchronous: they overlap, rollouts may be a few updates stale, and the ratio ρ with its clip (or a truncated importance weight) keeps the off-policy update honest.
