Q-learning: learn the optimal action values without a model
@@ 3
A one-line update that converges to the optimal action values with probability 1, however clumsily you explore, as long as you keep trying every action in every state.

@@ 5
Keep a table Q(s,a); after each step move Q(s,a) a little toward "reward plus γ times the best Q in the next state", and the table converges to q<sub>*</sub> "so long as all actions are repeatedly sampled in all states and the action-values are represented discretely". <a class="src" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00992698">Watkins &amp; Dayan 1992</a>
@@ 6
You are in a maze and stick a note on every door saying how good it is to go through it. Each time you walk through a door, you rewrite its note: "what I got on the other side, plus the best note I can see in the next room". You can wander randomly and still, after enough wandering, the notes become exactly right, and the best path is just "follow the biggest note".
@@ 7
Dynamic programming (Bellman 1957; Howard's policy iteration, 1960) finds optimal policies, but only given the full rulebook p(s′,r|s,a), and only for small problems: backgammon alone has about 10<sup>20</sup> states. <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a> Temporal-difference learning (Sutton 1988) learned without a model, but it learned the value of a fixed policy, and to act greedily from a state-value function you still need the model. <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/papers/sutton-88-with-erratum.pdf">Sutton 1988</a> Watkins's 1989 thesis reframed the whole problem as "learning to control a Markov Decision Process by incremental dynamic programming" and included a sketch of a convergence proof; the 1992 note with Dayan supplies the proof. <a class="src" href="https://www.cs.rhul.ac.uk/~chrisw/thesis.html">Watkins 1989</a> <a class="src" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00992698">Watkins &amp; Dayan 1992</a>
@@ 8
Learn <strong>action values</strong>, and learn the optimal ones directly. The target uses the <em>best</em> action in the next state, max<sub>a</sub> Q(S′,a), not the action you happen to take. So the table "directly approximates q<sub>*</sub>, the optimal action-value function, independent of the policy being followed". <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a> That is what <strong>off-policy</strong> means: the behaviour policy explores, the target policy is greedy, and the two never have to agree. Once Q is right, the policy is read off with no model and no search: act greedily.
@@ 9
Initialise Q(s,a) arbitrarily for every pair; set Q(terminal, ·) = 0.
@@ 10
In state S, choose A by any policy that keeps exploring, for example ε-greedy on Q.
@@ 11
Take A; observe reward R and next state S′.
@@ 12
Form the target: R + γ max<sub>a</sub> Q(S′, a). This is a one-step sample of the Bellman optimality equation.
@@ 13

@@ 14
Q(S<sub>t</sub>,A<sub>t</sub>) ← Q(S<sub>t</sub>,A<sub>t</sub>) + α [ R<sub>t+1</sub> + γ max<sub>a</sub> Q(S<sub>t+1</sub>,a) − Q(S<sub>t</sub>,A<sub>t</sub>) ]
@@ 15
S ← S′; repeat until the episode ends, then start another.
@@ 16
<strong>The convergence theorem, in words.</strong> Run this forever. If every state–action pair is updated infinitely often, the values are stored exactly (a table, one cell per pair), and the step sizes shrink in the usual stochastic-approximation way (they add up to infinity, their squares do not), then Q converges to q<sub>*</sub> with probability 1. <a class="src" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00992698">Watkins &amp; Dayan 1992</a> <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a> Nothing is assumed about <em>how</em> you choose actions. The note also sketches two extensions: undiscounted problems with absorbing goal states, and updating many Q values per step rather than one. <a class="src" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00992698">Watkins &amp; Dayan 1992</a>

@@ 32
This is a theory paper: the result is the theorem, not a benchmark. The behaviour it predicts is visible in Sutton and Barto's cliff-walking gridworld (Example 6.6, ε = 0.1): Q-learning learns the values of the optimal cliff-edge path, while the on-policy Sarsa learns a longer, safer route. Because exploration keeps pushing the agent off the cliff, Q-learning's online return is <em>worse</em>, about −50 per episode against Sarsa's about −25 in the book's plot; RL School's replication (α = 0.5, 50 runs) gives −50.9 and −27.4. <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a> The book also shows the max in the target causing <strong>maximisation bias</strong>: in Example 6.7 Q-learning keeps picking a bad action about 5% too often even at convergence, which Double Q-learning removes. <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a> <a class="src" href="https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2010/hash/091d584fced301b442654dd8c23b3fc9-Abstract.html">van Hasselt 2010</a>
@@ 33
Sutton and Barto date the moment the dynamic-programming and trial-and-error threads of RL were "fully brought together" to Watkins's 1989 thesis; the term "action value" is also his. <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a> Twenty-six years later the same update, with a convolutional network in place of the table, became DQN: 49 Atari games from pixels at a level "comparable to that of a professional human games tester" (Nature, February 2015). <a class="src" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/nature14236">Mnih 2015</a> Every value-based deep RL method since, Double DQN, Rainbow, Agent57, is this update plus engineering (see <a href="#p-dqn">the DQN review</a>).
@@ 34
The theorem needs a table. Replace it with a function approximator and you have all three ingredients of the <strong>deadly triad</strong> (approximation, bootstrapping, off-policy), under which instability "can" occur; "if any two elements of the deadly triad are present, but not all three", it can be avoided. <a class="src" href="http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf">Sutton &amp; Barto 2018</a> The max over noisy estimates overestimates: Double DQN (2015) observed overestimation in all 49 Atari games and lifted the median normalised score from 93.5% to 114.7% by fixing it. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.06461">van Hasselt 2015</a> The theorem gives no rate, and "infinitely often" is not a budget. And, as cliff walking shows, learning the optimal policy is not the same as behaving well while you learn.
