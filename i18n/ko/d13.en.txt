Every RL system that works outside a game has three ingredients: a place to try millions of things cheaply (usually a simulator), a reward you can measure, and an evaluation honest enough to separate skill from luck. Where a deployment is weak, it is weak on one of these. The algorithms are ones you know: PPO almost everywhere, an actor-critic for the tokamak, REINFORCE at YouTube, AlphaZero's search for algorithm discovery.
@@ 6
1. What a real deployment needs
@@ 7
In <a href="#d12">Day 12</a> the environment was a sandbox you could call a billion times. The physical world is not: a robot hand breaks, a tokamak shot lasts under three seconds and needs fifteen minutes to cool down, and a recommender's environment is two billion people. <a class="src" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04301-9">Degrave 2022</a> So ask three questions of every deployment story.
@@ 8
<strong>Where did the experience come from?</strong> Almost always a <strong>simulator</strong>; otherwise logged data, with its off-policy traps. <strong>What was the reward, and who measured it?</strong> "Energy saved", "view time" and "mortality" are a meter reading, a proxy, and an outcome nobody was allowed to randomise. <strong>How was skill separated from luck?</strong> Ten trials on one robot, a live A/B test over millions of users, and a retrospective match against clinicians' decisions carry very different weight.
@@ 9
Learning to ride a bike by falling off ten thousand times would hurt. So you practise on a video-game bike first, and you make the game bike a bit random, sometimes heavier, sometimes with a wobbly wheel, so that when you ride the real one, whatever it does, you have already met something like it.

2. Robots I: sim-to-real and domain randomisation
@@ 11
OpenAI's <strong>Dactyl</strong> (August 2018) taught a 24-joint Shadow Hand to rotate a block to a target orientation, in-hand, from simulation alone. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.00177">OpenAI 2018</a> <strong>State</strong>: 15 fingertip coordinates, the block's position and the target orientation, all noisy. <strong>Action</strong>: 20 relative joint targets, each discretised into 11 bins. <strong>Reward</strong>: the decrease in angle to the target, +5 on reaching it, −20 for dropping the block. Algorithm: PPO (clip 0.2, γ = 0.998, GAE λ = 0.95) with a 512-unit LSTM, on 6,144 CPU cores and 8 GPUs, about 22 years of simulated hand time per hour. <a class="src" href="https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1808.00177">OpenAI 2018</a>
@@ 12
The <strong>sim-to-real gap</strong>: friction, motor delays, camera colours and cable stretch are all slightly wrong in the simulator, and a policy tuned to its exact values fails at the first real contact. The fix is <strong>domain randomisation</strong>: train on thousands of simulators at once, each with different physics, appearance, noise and unmodelled forces. Since the policy cannot see which simulator it is in, it needs memory; the LSTM infers the dynamics from the first few steps. <a class="src" href="https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1808.00177">OpenAI 2018</a>
@@ 13
The ablations are the lesson. On the real hand (10 trials) the full system managed a median of 13 consecutive rotations, max 50. Without randomisation: 0. Without physics randomisation: 2. Feed-forward instead of LSTM: 3. In simulation the same policy scored 50, so 50 versus 13 is the gap in one number. The price: about 100 years of simulated experience (50 hours) to converge, against 3 years (1.5 hours) without. <a class="src" href="https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1808.00177">OpenAI 2018</a>
@@ 14
Try it: a one-dimensional version of the same problem. Set a randomisation range, train, deploy, and watch what the hidden real friction does.
@@ 15
Notice that with range 0 or 0.05 real success is 0%: the real friction (0.62) lands in a bucket training never visited, so the policy pushes with the simulator's default force and stops short. From ±0.15 upward it is 100%, but the episodes needed to reach 90% rise from 120 to about 250 as the range widens, because the same budget is spread over more buckets. Dactyl's 3 years versus 100, in miniature.
@@ 16
The 2019 sequel, <strong>Solving Rubik's Cube with a Robot Hand</strong>, learned the ranges. <strong>Automatic Domain Randomisation (ADR)</strong> starts from one environment and widens each range whenever performance at its edge passes a threshold: an open-ended curriculum. Same reward shape, a 1,024-unit LSTM, PPO on 64 V100s and 920 × 32 CPU cores, roughly 13,000 simulated years. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.07113">OpenAI 2019</a> Results, 10 trials each: with the cube's built-in sensor supplying face angles, 60% on 15-rotation scrambles and 20% on the hardest 26-rotation ones; from vision alone, 20% and 0%. <a class="src" href="https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.07113">OpenAI 2019</a>
@@ 17
<strong>"RL solved the Rubik's cube."</strong> The move sequence came from a classical solver (Kociemba). RL learned the finger motions, and finished the hardest scrambles 20% of the time, with a sensor inside the cube. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.07113">OpenAI 2019</a>
@@ 18
<strong>"Sim-to-real is a compute problem."</strong> Randomisation and memory moved Dactyl's real median from 0 to 13; compute mostly paid for the randomisation. <a class="src" href="https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1808.00177">OpenAI 2018</a>
@@ 19
<strong>"The simulator must match reality."</strong> It only needs to contain reality somewhere inside its range, which is why ADR works without anyone measuring the real friction.

3. Robots II: massively parallel simulation
@@ 21
Dactyl ran physics on CPUs and shipped observations to GPUs. <strong>Isaac Gym</strong> (NVIDIA, August 2021) put the physics on the GPU too: "tens of thousands of simultaneous environments on a single GPU", 2–3 orders of magnitude faster than CPU pipelines, with sweet spots of 8,192 environments for Ant, 4,096 for Humanoid and ANYmal, 16,384 for the Shadow Hand. A Humanoid reaches reward 5,000 in under 4 minutes on one A100; the Shadow Hand cube task reaches 20 consecutive successes in under 35 minutes, where Dactyl needed 50 hours on a cluster. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.10470">Makoviychuk 2021</a>
@@ 22
Rudin et al. made a quadruped walk in "under four minutes" on flat ground and twenty minutes on rough terrain (1,500 updates) with 4,096 simulated ANYmals on one RTX A6000, then deployed on the real ANYmal C unchanged. <strong>State</strong>: base velocities, gravity direction, joint positions and velocities, the previous action, 108 terrain heights around the body. <strong>Action</strong>: joint targets for a PD controller. <strong>Reward</strong>: nine weighted terms, tracking the commanded velocity, penalising torques, joint accelerations, action changes, body wobble and collisions, rewarding feet-in-air time. Caveat: the lidar height map was imperfect on hardware, so the real robot was capped at 0.6 m/s. <a class="src" href="https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2109.11978">Rudin 2021</a>
@@ 23
Since then: ANYmal parkour at 2 m/s (June 2023). <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14874">Hoeller 2023</a> A 20-joint humanoid footballer walking 181% faster and turning 302% faster than a scripted controller, zero-shot (April 2023). <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13653">Haarnoja 2023</a> Humanoid locomotion for Unitree G1 and Booster T1 in "just 15 minutes with a single RTX 4090 GPU", with fast off-policy SAC/TD3 recipes (December 2025). <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.01996">Seo 2025</a> A Unitree Go1 at 2.0 m/s zero-shot from Isaac (July 2026). <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18135">Dowdy 2026</a> The algorithm family barely changed; the simulator moved from a cluster to a card, and days became a quarter of an hour.
@@ 24
Dactyl was a driving school with 6,000 instructors phoning results to one examiner. Isaac Gym is 4,096 driving simulators bolted to the examiner's desk. The student (PPO) did not get smarter; the school got faster.

Two designs recur. RT-2 writes actions as text so one Transformer does everything, at 1–3 Hz. π0 keeps a stock VLM and attaches an <strong>action expert</strong> that denoises a chunk of 50 future actions by <strong>flow matching</strong>, a cousin of <a href="#p-diffusion-policy">Diffusion Policy</a>, for smooth 50 Hz control. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2410.24164">Black 2024</a>
@@ 52
Imitation has a ceiling: the policy is at best as good as its demonstrations and has never seen its own mistakes. RL fills that gap, and 2025–26 is when it was filled for VLAs. The obstacle: PPO needs π<sub>new</sub>(a|s)/π<sub>old</sub>(a|s), and a flow-matching head has no tractable likelihood for the chunk it produced. <strong>πRL</strong> (October 2025) gives two fixes. <em>Flow-Noise</em> treats the 10 denoising steps as a small MDP with a learned noise scale, so each step has a Gaussian density and the chain an exact log-likelihood. <em>Flow-SDE</em> turns the deterministic ODE into a stochastic one. Either way PPO then runs. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.25889">Chen 2025</a> From few-shot imitation, π0.5 on LIBERO goes 77.1% → 98.3% (Flow-Noise), π0 57.6% → 97.6%, and on ManiSkill's 4,352 pick-and-place combinations π0.5 40.1% → 90.9%. Full-dataset imitation reached 94.2% and 96.9%, so RL from a handful of demos beat imitation from all of them. All in simulation. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2510.25889">Chen 2025</a>
@@ 53
Try it: step through one VLA episode, then switch on RL fine-tuning.
@@ 54
Notice which box the gradient arrow reaches: the action head only. The 3B backbone that knows what a bowl is stays frozen, so RL teaches dexterity without erasing language.
@@ 55
Two 2026 papers carried this to real robots. <strong>RL-Co</strong> (February 2026): imitation on mixed real and simulated demos, then RL in ManiSkill3 with a supervised loss on the real demos so the policy keeps real-world appearance. On four real tabletop tasks OpenVLA went 9.1% (real-only imitation) → 40.0% (sim-real co-training) → 64.0% (RL-Co); π0.5 26.7% → 45.9% → 66.1%, with 20–50 real demos per task and 20–64 trials per checkpoint. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12628">Shi 2026</a> <strong>Generative 3D worlds</strong> (CoRL 2026) attacks the missing simulator: GPT-4o designs scene graphs, a generative pipeline builds 100 interactive scenes from 516 assets (about 47 minutes each), and π0 is fine-tuned in them with a PPO variant plus randomisation. Real WidowX pick-and-place with distractors rose 21.7% → 75% over 240 trials, and success scaled with the number of scenes. One robot, one task family, one camera. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18532">Choi 2026</a>

5. Chips: AlphaChip
@@ 57
Placing a chip's large blocks (<strong>macros</strong>) is a game with one delayed reward. In Google's 2020–21 work, since named <strong>AlphaChip</strong>: <strong>state</strong> = a graph embedding of the netlist (what is placed and where), the next block, metadata, and a mask of cells where it fits; <strong>action</strong> = the grid cell (up to 128 × 128, typically 30 × 30) for that block; <strong>reward</strong> = zero until the end, then −(approximate wirelength + 0.01 × congestion), with density capped at 0.6 by the mask. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.10746">Mirhoseini 2020</a> Algorithm: PPO with an <strong>edge-based graph convolutional</strong> encoder, first trained as a supervised predictor of placement quality on 10,000 placements of 5 netlists, then as a policy on 20 TPU blocks, then fine-tuned on new blocks. <a class="src" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03544-w">Mirhoseini 2021</a>
@@ 58
Nature (June 2021): placements "in under six hours" matching or beating months of human effort; one example has 55.42 m of wire against the human 57.07 m. Against the academic placer RePlAce, RL wins wirelength on two of five blocks; RePlAce wins the other three but breaks the 1% congestion limit on some. <a class="src" href="https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.10746">Mirhoseini 2020</a> DeepMind's September 2024 blog says it has been used in every TPU generation since 2020, including v5e, v5p and Trillium, that MediaTek extended it, and that a checkpoint and code are public. <a class="src" href="https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/how-alphachip-transformed-computer-chip-design/">DeepMind 2024</a>
@@ 59
The other side. Cheng, Kahng and colleagues (2023, updated March 2026) re-implemented the open-source version, report a well-tuned simulated-annealing baseline stronger than the paper's comparisons suggest, and call the scalability and pre-training claims "still-missing confirmations", since the TPU training blocks are private. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.11014">Cheng 2023</a> Google's reply is a Nature Addendum (September 2024) that names the method and points to the blog, where per-generation use is a bar chart, not a stated count. <a class="src" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08032-5">Mirhoseini 2024</a> Undisputed: the MDP is sound and the method ran in production. Also undisputed: no outside group has reproduced the headline comparison on the original data.

6. Fusion: steering a plasma 10,000 times a second
@@ 61
A tokamak's coils hold a 100-million-degree plasma away from the walls. DeepMind and the Swiss Plasma Center (Nature, February 2022) replaced the hand-designed controller on the TCV tokamak with a learned one. <strong>State</strong>: 34 flux loops, 38 magnetic probes, 19 coil currents, and the targets. <strong>Action</strong>: a voltage for each of the 19 coils every 100 µs, a 10 kHz loop with about 50 µs of compute per step. <strong>Reward</strong>: quality terms in [0, 1] for plasma current, shape and position, combined and normalised so a perfect second scores 100, with a large penalty if the plasma is lost. <a class="src" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04301-9">Degrave 2022</a>
@@ 62
Algorithm: <strong>MPO</strong> (maximum a posteriori policy optimisation), an off-policy actor-critic in <a href="#p-sac">SAC</a>'s family, trained only in a plasma simulator with 5,000 actors for 1–3 days. The design is <strong>asymmetric</strong>: the critic is a 256-unit LSTM (about 718k parameters) that never leaves the lab; the deployed actor is an MLP of about 266k parameters that fits the 50 µs budget. It transferred zero-shot. Results: plasma-current error 0.62 kA RMS and shape error 0.75 cm; an elongation of 1.9 (unstable, growing at over 1.4 kHz) held steady; negative triangularity of −0.8; a snowflake whose X-points were moved from 34 cm apart to 6.6 cm; and two separate "droplet" plasmas sustained together for the full 200 ms window, a first. <a class="src" href="https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/accelerating-fusion-science-through-learned-plasma-control/">DeepMind 2022</a>
@@ 63
The authors' caveats: steady-state errors in elongation, a simulator without heat and current transport, no guarantee against disruptions (a machine-protection layer is recommended), and a conventional controller still handling breakdown and ramp-up. <a class="src" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04301-9">Degrave 2022</a> What made it tractable: a good simulator, a dense physical reward, no humans in the loop.

7. Data-centre cooling: a lesson in reading claims
@@ 65
You will hear that "DeepMind's RL cut Google's data-centre cooling energy by 40%". Read the two primary posts. July 2016: "up to 40 percent" less energy for cooling and a 15 percent reduction in overall PUE overhead, at one data centre, using ensembles of neural networks trained on historical sensor data to predict future PUE and temperatures, with recommendations checked against constraints. <a class="src" href="https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/deepmind-ai-reduces-google-data-centre-cooling-bill-by-40/">DeepMind 2016</a> August 2018: the system takes direct control under operator supervision, every five minutes screening candidate actions against safety constraints with two-layer verification and human override, for "consistent energy savings of around 30 percent on average", up from about 12% at launch. <a class="src" href="https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/safety-first-ai-for-autonomous-data-centre-cooling-and-industrial-control/">DeepMind 2018</a>
@@ 66
Neither post calls this reinforcement learning; "predict the consequences of candidate actions, pick the best that passes the constraints" is prediction plus optimisation. Good engineering, with a safety architecture worth copying. But "RL saves 40%" fails all three of section 1's questions: cooling energy, not total; one site; not RL. Find the primary source; check the denominator, the scope and the method.

8. Recommenders: REINFORCE at YouTube scale
@@ 68
The one large consumer deployment with a published RL objective is YouTube's "Top-K Off-Policy Correction for a REINFORCE Recommender System" (WSDM, February 2019). <strong>State</strong>: a user vector from a recurrent network over the user's history of recommendations and reactions, plus context (page, device, time). <strong>Action</strong>: one video from millions; a page shows K = 16. <strong>Reward</strong>: engagement with that item (zero if not clicked), aggregated over a 4–10 hour horizon. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02353">Chen 2019</a>
@@ 69
Two corrections make <a href="#p-reinforce">REINFORCE</a> work on logged data. The data came from other recommenders, so each gradient term is weighted by π(a|s)/β(a|s), with β estimated by a separate softmax head and the weight capped at e³. And because K items are shown together, item a's gradient is scaled by λ<sub>K</sub>, which stops the policy piling probability onto an item that will be on the page anyway. <a class="src" href="https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1812.02353">Chen 2019</a>
@@ 70
Live A/B: the top-K correction added 0.85% view time over the standard correction; a K = 1 model was 0.66% worse than K = 16; raising the cap to e⁵ cost 0.52%. <a class="src" href="https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1812.02353">Chen 2019</a> The caveats are in the MDP: fractions of a percent (large at YouTube's scale, tiny per user), engagement as the reward, a horizon of hours. This is one-step REINFORCE with a learned state, not long-horizon planning.

10. Driving, healthcare, finance: three cautions
@@ 92
<strong>Driving.</strong> The verifiable result is Waymo-affiliated: "Imitation Is Not Enough" (December 2022) trains behaviour cloning plus RL on over 100k miles of urban driving logs and reports over 38% fewer failures on the hardest scenarios than imitation alone. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.11419">Lu 2022</a> Any claim that a deployed Waymo or Tesla stack is "trained with RL" is, as of October 2026, unsupported by a primary source.
@@ 93
<strong>Healthcare.</strong> The AI Clinician (Nature Medicine, October 2018) learned a sepsis treatment policy from ICU records by offline RL; "mortality was lowest in patients for whom clinicians' actual doses matched the AI decisions". <a class="src" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-018-0213-5">Komorowski 2018</a> A 2019 critique examined the individual treatment profiles it recommended and the evaluation's assumptions. <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.03271">Jeter 2019</a> Section 1's third question again: observational data allows only off-policy evaluation, "clinician agreed with the policy" is confounded with easier patients, and the policy was never deployed. Lower mortality in the agreeing subgroup is not a mortality reduction.
@@ 94
<strong>Finance.</strong> A 2021 survey covers RL for execution, portfolio choice, hedging and market making with methods that "do not require any model assumptions". <a class="src" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.04553">Hambly 2021</a> The caution is the environment: markets are non-stationary and adversarial, data per regime is small, and a backtest is one trajectory, not a simulator you can re-sample.
@@ 95
Going deeper (optional): why offline evaluation is the hard part
@@ 96
Every deployment without a simulator must estimate how a new policy would do from data gathered by an old one, using importance weights (YouTube's π/β) or a learned model. Both have high variance exactly where the new policy differs most from the old, which is where the gains are. The cap at e³, and the 0.52% lost by raising it to e⁵, is that trade-off measured live. <a class="src" href="https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1812.02353">Chen 2019</a> Distrust an offline-RL result in proportion to how far its policy strays from the data.

Q3. Why is PPO hard to apply to a flow-matching action head, and how does πRL get around it?
@@ 168
The chunk's log-likelihood is intractable, so π_new/π_old cannot be computed; πRL treats each denoising step as a Gaussian action so the chain has an exact likelihood
@@ 169
Flow heads output discrete tokens, so clipping does not apply; πRL switches to REINFORCE
@@ 170
The VLM backbone must be unfrozen, which is too expensive; πRL distils it to 300M parameters
@@ 171
The reward is continuous, so GAE diverges; πRL binarises it
@@ 172
Flow-Noise models the 10 denoising steps as a small MDP with a learned noise scale; Flow-SDE converts the ODE to an SDE. Both recover a PPO ratio. The backbone stays frozen and the reward is already 0/1.
